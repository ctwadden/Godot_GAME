# Payments & Tokens — full narration transcript

Recorded narration and captions use these exact paragraphs. Optional questions follow each explanation.

## 1. Currency and payment method

### What happens

A book costs ten fictional credits. A customer could hand over cash, use a card, or make an account transfer. The payment method changes, but the price is still measured in the same currency.

### Why it happens

Currency is the unit used to express value. A payment method is the route used to transfer it. A card is not a separate currency, and paying electronically does not automatically mean using cryptocurrency.

### The impact

Customers and shops need to distinguish the price from the ways they can pay it. A shop might accept one route and refuse another, even when both use the same currency.

Optional question: The same ten-credit book is paid for by card instead of cash. What changed?

## 2. Cash changes hands

### What happens

The customer hands ten credits in physical cash to the shop. The customer no longer holds those notes or coins. The shop now holds them. We are illustrating one handover, not repeating purchases.

### Why it happens

The cashier checks the amount and any change. The cash handover does not require an online account update. The shop may make a receipt, but that record is separate from the physical cash.

### The impact

Cash can remain usable during an internet outage. However, the shop needs change, safe storage and handling. A customer who brought no cash cannot use this route. Cash has practical costs even without an electronic transaction fee.

Optional question: The shop's internet fails. Must a cash handover stop?

## 3. An account payment updates records

### What happens

In our central-account example, the customer authorizes a ten-credit transfer. A payment provider checks the request. The customer balance changes from twenty to ten, and the shop balance changes from zero to ten.

### Why it happens

The provider checks authorization, available funds and the transfer rules before updating its ledger. A ledger is a system of transaction or balance records. Here, no physical note travels from customer to shop.

### The impact

Electronic records can make payments convenient to process and reconcile. Access depends on the account, accepted payment instrument and required infrastructure. A flat phone can block a mobile payment, but it does not necessarily block a physical card.

Optional question: What moved in this account-payment illustration?

## 4. A shared ledger has several copies

### What happens

Now several computers maintain copies of a shared transaction record. In this classroom example, at least two of three authorized computers must agree before an update is accepted. This is a toy rule, not a real payment network.

### Why it happens

The computers follow agreed rules for validating and recording an update. Blockchain is one way of organizing shared records. Different networks use different agreement methods; they do not all use proof of work.

### The impact

Sharing records changes the dependencies and who must be trusted. One unavailable computer need not stop this example, but losing enough computers can. Shared records do not guarantee privacy: aliases can still be linked to people.

Optional question: One computer fails in this toy two-of-three system. Two remain available. What follows?

## 5. Cryptocurrency is not the ledger itself

### What happens

A cryptocurrency is a digital asset that can be transferred through a network. The ledger records a transfer of that asset. What is transferred and the record of its transfer are related, but different things.

### Why it happens

The network's rules govern authorization and updates. A shared ledger can also record things other than currency. Ordinary electronic payments do not become cryptocurrency payments simply because computers are involved.

### The impact

Being transferable does not guarantee that a shop will accept an asset or that its value will remain stable. Network operation, payment acceptance and financial risk are different questions. This lesson makes no investment recommendation.

Optional question: Does every electronic payment use cryptocurrency?

## 6. A copied file is not a copied token

### What happens

The same image file can appear in three folders. Separately, our fictional register contains one token identifier: classroom collection, zero zero zero one. The file copies and that unique entry are different objects.

### Why it happens

Copying image bytes does not create another entry in this register. Changing the recorded token controller from Alias A to Alias B changes control of the existing entry. The collection and token identifier stay the same.

### The impact

Possessing an image copy does not establish control of its token. Controlling the token also does not, by itself, establish that a real-world authorship claim is true. We must inspect what the record actually establishes.

Optional question: An image is copied into three folders. Does that alone create three token entries?

## 7. A licence explains permission

### What happens

Read our fictional licence card. It permits classroom display with credit to Cedar Studio. It does not permit commercial reproduction, and the creator retains copyright. Those terms are separate from the token-controller label.

### Why it happens

A file copy shows possession of bytes. A token entry shows recorded control. Permission to use the associated work comes from the applicable terms. In this example, transferring the token does not change the licence.

### The impact

A recipient cannot infer permission to sell printed copies just from controlling the token. A different explicit licence could grant different rights. This is a conceptual comparison using fictional terms, not legal advice about a real asset.

Optional question: Under the displayed fictional licence, does token control alone permit selling printed copies?

## 8. A record does not preserve linked media

### What happens

The token record points to an image on an external host. Imagine that host becomes unavailable. The pointer and token record remain, but a new request cannot retrieve the linked image. No real website is contacted in this demonstration.

### Why it happens

A reference to a file is not the file itself. Keeping the token record and keeping the media available are different responsibilities. Local copies already held in the classroom can survive the failure of that external host.

### The impact

A persistent token record does not automatically promise permanent media access. A recipient needs to consider storage and continuing availability, not just the existence of the record. A broken link does not erase every existing copy.

Optional question: The external host disappears. What can still remain?

## 9. Cashless access depends on the customer

### What happens

A shop accepts only its selected mobile account payment. One customer has an account and a charged phone. Another has no accepted account. Another has a flat phone. Another needs an accessible payment interface.

### Why it happens

These are different barriers. A working internet connection alone does not provide an account, charge a phone or make an interface accessible. A physical card, assisted service or another payment route could change a particular customer's situation.

### The impact

The shop may gain convenient records, while some customers lose access. Offering cash as well can help customers who carry it, but adds handling and infrastructure. A recommendation should name the shop, different customers and service providers.

Optional question: Why is there no universal best payment policy?

## 10. Small purchases add up

### What happens

Five fixed-price digital items cost two fake dollars each. The cumulative cost is two, four, six, eight and then ten dollars. Five times two equals ten. This is a static worksheet, not a shop.

### Why it happens

A small price per item does not remove the cumulative cost. A disclosed fixed item tells the buyer what they receive. A random-reward offer adds uncertainty about the item. We do not run a draw or show rewards here.

### The impact

Buyers need to understand total spending, contents or probabilities, duplicates and restrictions. A publisher's interest in repeat spending may conflict with a buyer's or parent's interest in controlling costs. Small individual prices still deserve scrutiny.

Optional question: Five disclosed items cost two fake dollars each. What is the total?

## 11. E-trading involves access, rules and risk

### What happens

E-trading means buying and selling financial products through digital platforms. The chapter's historical GameStop case describes wider participation, platform restrictions and financial exposure. We are analysing the account, not simulating trades or predicting prices.

### Why it happens

A platform can make participation easier while setting conditions on access. Digital access does not remove financial risk. Evaluating a restriction requires evidence about the situation, the platform's responsibilities and the people affected.

### The impact

Ask who can participate, who can change the rules and who could lose money. Compare perspectives and seek dated primary evidence before judging the case. Completing a classroom model would not demonstrate an ability to predict real markets.

Optional question: What should we examine in the chapter's e-trading case?
